# The Provinces of the Commonwealth of Laphurdeen

Under Article 41 of the Constitution, the territory of the Commonwealth is
divided into provinces - no fewer than fifteen, no more than eighteen -
established by Act of Parliament on the recommendation of the independent
Boundary Commission, having regard to community, geography, and reasonable
equality of population. The Commission's map, in force since the Third
Boundary Act, draws **sixteen**.

Each province elects three members of the Commons. Government follows
subsidiarity: decisions are taken as close to the people affected as good
government allows. Two cities hold national charters under Article 41(3) -
**Lapentieur**, the capital, within the National Capital Region, and
**Darlingmoors**, the Commonwealth's largest city, within Darcambria - each
with its own elected council and mayor inside its province.

The names on the map tell the settlement story plainly: English names in
the majority, as the English were the majority of the pre-Laphurdeen
settlers; Laphurdi names where the Reform-era Commission set the national
language on the land; and French and Norse blends where those threads came
ashore. Like the ⟨ph⟩ of Laphurdeen, the English and French names were
never touched by the Reform.

## The Sixteen

### Darcambria - seat: Darlingmoors

**Etymology:** *dar* ("amber," the Charter-era blend of French *doré* and
English *amber*) + *cambria* (from French *cambrer*, "to curve"): **"the
amber curve."** Once the name of the harbour city, now the name of the
province whose great curved bay - the founding Anchorage - carries the
flag's amber arc.

The metropolitan province: around the bay stand the eight Charter cities -
Darlingmoors, Addison, Marionberry, Upperlea, Lowerlea, Briarside,
Winchester-on-the-Sea, and Riverlin - governing together as the Darcambrian
Communities, with the Government Centre on Darlingmoors island. Population
4,900,000, the better half of it in Darlingmoors alone. See `DARCAMBRIA.md`.

### National Capital Region - seat: Lapentieur

**Etymology:** the one name on the map that is pure statute - the Boundary
Commission's own English, unadorned, for the province that belongs to the
whole Commonwealth rather than to any one settlement thread.

The fog-brushed hill country around the capital. Lapentieur ("the place of
slopes") holds Parliament, the ministries, and the Founders' Hills - the
Grundarkulleren. Population 1,250,000.

### Southcape - seat: Agaetisboro

**Etymology:** English *south* + *cape*, the headland the first fishers
rounded to find sun the whole year - and sent back the single word
*«Agaet»* that named their town.

The holiday south: white sand, coral reefs and coves, the casino coast.
Agaetisboro is the Commonwealth's sunniest city and its greatest
understatement.

### Peaklands - seat: Highbridge

**Etymology:** English *peak* + *lands*, the settlers' plain name for the
range whose gorges and summits rise behind the amber curve.

The mountain province. Highbridge takes its name from the Charter-era stone
span across the Inner Gorge headwaters; the high paths look down on
Darlingmoors' Peak District, which borrowed the range's name for a city
neighbourhood.

### Easthaven - seat: Oesthamn

**Etymology:** English *east* + *haven*, "the eastern harbour" - the first
safe water the fleet found on the sunrise coast. The province keeps the
settlers' name, never touched by the Reform; the harbour town answers in
Laphurdi, for the Commission set the exact calque on the quays: *oest*
("east") + *hamn* ("harbour"), **Oesthamn** - the same harbour, said in
the national language.

Fishing coast and morning markets: the catch lands before dawn and the
province eats it by noon. Baaner Laphurdeen's eastern line ends on the
quays of Oesthamn.

### Torkadeland - seat: Hedeport

**Etymology:** *torka* ("to dry") in its participle, + *land*: **"the
parched land."** The English called it Westmoor; the Reform-era Commission
set the Laphurdi name on the heath, and the dry-stone walls kept standing
under both. The seat followed the province: the settlers' Moorgate - the
town at the old gate onto the moor - became **Hedeport**, *hede*
("heath") + *port* ("gate"): the same gate, named from the Laphurdi side.

Heath, thyme, and dry-stone country in the west's rain shadow. Hedeport
keeps the province's market and its patience.

### Lakmorgen - seat: Whitmere

**Etymology:** *lak* ("lake") + *morgen* ("morning"): **"the morning
lake,"** for the hour when the water lies stillest and the whole province
is a mirror.

The lake district. Whitmere - English, "the white mere" - sits on the
largest of the lakes and answers the province's Laphurdi name with its own
settler one: two languages, one water.

### Rullarkuller - seat: Kornhem

**Etymology:** *rulla* ("to roll") + *kuller* ("hills," the plural of
*kulle* - cf. Grundarkulleren, the Founders' Hills of Lapentieur): **"the
rolling hills."** The English settlers called this country the Wolds and
its town Woldham; the Commission set Laphurdi on both - the province named
for the hills, and the seat for what the hills carry: *korn* ("grain") +
*hem* ("home"), **Kornhem**, "grain-home."

Rolling grain country inland of the east coast. Kornhem holds the grain
exchange and the harvest festival; the eastern regional line calls at
Kornhem on its way to the coast at Oesthamn.

### Vindalen - seat: Vindalen

**Etymology:** *vin* ("wine") + *dal* ("valley"), with the old definite
*-en* - **"the wine dale,"** the same ending that closes *Saltmarsken*
and *Laphurdeen* itself. The English settlers called it Vinedale; the
Reform-era Commission set the Laphurdi name on the map, and the vines
climbed on regardless.

Terraced vineyards on the southern hills, the krona-a-glass harvest
festivals, and the long lunch the rest of the Commonwealth pretends to
disapprove of.

### The Forests - seat: Pineforest

**Etymology:** English, plural and definite, the settlers' name for the
one part of the map that needed no invention: the forests.

Cork-oak and pine from valley to ridge. Pineforest began as a logging
camp at the crossing of the west road; the province now cuts less than it
plants, and the cork harvest is a decennial festival.

### Mund-av-Lin - seat: Linmouth

**Etymology:** *mund* ("mouth") + *av* ("of") + **Lin**, the river:
**"the mouth of the Lin"** - the same river whose far shore named the
Charter city of Riverlin. The English town kept its own name for the same
geography: Linmouth.

The delta province: reed channels, eel weirs, and the river pilots who
walk cargo up the shifting mouth. Linbridge, upstream, is the last dry
crossing.

### Saltmarsken - seat: Saltvik

**Etymology:** *salt* + *marsk* ("salt marsh"), with the old definite
*-en* - **"the salt marsh,"** the same archaic ending that closes
*Laphurdeen* itself. The seat is *Saltvik*, "salt cove," where the pans
meet the sea.

Salt pans, lagoons, and the flamingos that winter on them. The salt of
Saltvik seasons the whole Commonwealth's table and always has; the old
pan-workers' cottages are the province's postcard.

### Highcliffe - seat: Highcliffe

**Etymology:** English *high* + *cliffe*, the settler spelling kept
un-Reformed: the chalk wall the north coast shows the sea.

The chalk cliffs of the north coast, the lighthouses on them, and the
gull colonies between. Highcliffe town leans over its own harbour stair,
and the cliff path is the Commonwealth's best long walk.

### Sainte-Agathe - seat: Shellharbour

**Etymology:** French, from the settler chapel of Sainte-Agathe raised on
the largest of the outer isles - the French fleet's farthest anchorage,
never touched by the Reform. The English-named seat, Shellharbour, tells
the other half of the settlement story in a single word.

The scattered outer isles: chapel bells, shell-sand harbours, and the
slow boats between. Reached by Baaner Laphurdeen's integrated rail-ferry
sailings from the mainland - one ticket, boat included.

### Belhaven - seat: Belhaven

**Etymology:** French *bel* ("fair") + English *haven* - a Charter-era
blend in the manner of Beaulieu: **"the fair harbour."**

The west's deep-water port and its most bilingual table. Belhaven's
seafront runs French shutters over English brick, and the evening
promenade is constitutional in the older sense of the word.

### The Northern Straits - seat: Sundby

**Etymology:** English, for the water rather than the land: the province
is the straits, and the towns are its crossings. The seat, *Sundby* -
Norse *sund* ("strait") + *-by* ("town") - keeps the name the ferry
families brought with them.

The northern strait and its islands: ferry towns, herring smokehouses,
and the winter lights. Baaner Laphurdeen's rail-ferry sailings cross from
Sundby; the timetable is the province's calendar.

## The Country Towns

The smaller stops on Baaner Laphurdeen's regional lines, English-named
like most of the map:

- **Ashcombe** - Torkadeland; the dry valley under the ash ridge.
- **Fernlea** - Rullarkuller; a grain village in the fern meadow, cousin
  by name to Upperlea and Lowerlea on the Darcambrian bay.
- **Millford** - Lakmorgen; the mill at the lake-country ford.
- **Linbridge** - Mund-av-Lin; the last dry crossing of the Lin.
